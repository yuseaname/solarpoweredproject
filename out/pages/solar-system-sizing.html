Solar System Sizing: A Complete Step-by-Step Guide for Off-Grid DIYers

Avoid the two most expensive mistakes in solar DIY: over-buying unnecessary capacity that drains your budget, or under-sizing your system and running out of power in the middle of the night. Learn the precise math required to build a reliable, balanced solar setup.

Quick Summary: To size a solar system, you must first calculate your daily energy consumption in kilowatt-hours (kWh). Once you have your total "load," you divide that number by your local "peak sun hours" and apply a derating factor (typically 0.75–0.80) to account for real-world energy losses. This gives you the required solar array size in kilowatts (kW).

Sizing a solar system is a "Goldilocks" problem. If your system is too small, you won't have enough energy to run essential appliances during cloudy spells or throughout the night. If it is too large, you have wasted significant upfront capital on panels and batteries that your home will never actually utilize.

Whether you are designing a small off-grid cabin, a van conversion, or a large residential backup system, the fundamental principles of sizing remain the same. This guide will walk you through the math, the variables, and the practical steps to ensure your system is perfectly balanced for your specific energy needs.

The Three Pillars of Solar Sizing

A functional solar setup is an ecosystem of three distinct components. To achieve a reliable system, you cannot size one without considering the others.

1. The Solar Array (Generation)

Think of the array as your "engine." Its job is to produce enough kilowatt-hours (kWh) every single day to cover your daily consumption and simultaneously recharge your batteries. We measure this capacity in kilowatts (kW) or watts (W).

2. The Battery Bank (Storage)

The battery bank is your "fuel tank." It stores the energy produced during peak daylight hours so you can use it when the sun is down or obscured by clouds. We measure this capacity in kilowatt-hours (kWh) or Amp-hours (Ah).

3. The Inverter (Conversion)

The inverter is the "translator." It converts the DC (Direct Current) electricity from your panels and batteries into the AC (Alternating Current) electricity used by standard household appliances. Its capacity is measured by the maximum wattage it can handle at a single moment.

The 4-Staged Sizing Process

Step 1: Audit Your Daily Energy Load

Before looking at solar panels, you must look at your electricity usage. If you are connecting to an existing utility, review your last 12 months of bills to find your average daily kWh usage. If you are building an off-grid system from scratch, you must manually create a "load list."

Pro Tip: Don't just list the wattage; you must list how many hours each device runs per day. A 1,000W heater running for 1 hour uses much less energy than a 100W light bulb running for 12 hours.

Appliance Watts (W) Hours/Day Daily Total (Wh)
LED Lights (4 bulbs) 40W 5 hrs 200 Wh
Laptop 60W 4 hrs 240 Wh
Refrigerator (Avg.) 1500W (Peak) ~1 hr (active) 1500 Wh
Total Daily Load: 1,940 Wh (or 1.94 kWh)

Step 2: Determine Your Peak Sun Hours (PSH)

A common mistake is assuming "12 hours of daylight" equals "12 hours of solar production." Solar panels only produce at near-peak capacity when the sun is directly overhead. You need to find your local Peak Sun Hours (PSH). This is a measurement of solar intensity, not just daylight duration.

You can find this data using tools like the NREL PVWatts calculator. A desert region might get 6 PSH, while a cloudy coastal or northern region might only get 3 PSH.

Step 3: Calculate the Array Size (The Formula)

To find out how many kilowatts (kW) of solar panels you need, use the following formula. We include a "derating factor" (0.75) to account for real-world energy losses like dust on panels, wiring resistance, and heat-induced inefficiency.

The Solar Array Formula:
(Daily kWh ÷ Peak Sun Hours) ÷ 0.75 = Required Solar kW

Applying the Example:
If your load is 1.94 kWh and your location averages 4 Peak Sun Hours:
(1.94 ÷ 4) ÷ 0.75 = 0.64 kW (or 640 Watts)

Step 4: Sizing Your Battery Bank

Your battery needs to be large enough to power your load through the night and provide a buffer for consecutive cloudy days. This is often called "days of autonomy."

When sizing, you must consider the Depth of Discharge (DoD). You should never drain a battery to 0%.

  • Lead-Acid (AGM/Gel): Aim for 50% maximum discharge. If you need 1.94 kWh of usable energy, you actually need a 3.88 kWh battery bank.
  • Lithium (LiFePO4): You can safely use 80–90% of the capacity. For a 1.94 kWh load, a 2.2 kWh battery bank would suffice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if I add an Electric Vehicle (EV) later?

Adding an EV will drastically increase your daily kWh load. If you plan to charge a car, you should size your wiring, charge controller, and array for that future capacity now. Retrofitting a solar system for an EV is significantly more expensive than building it correctly the first time.

Does temperature affect solar sizing?

Yes. Solar panels actually become less efficient as they get hotter. While they produce more energy in bright sunlight, extreme heat can drop the voltage. This is why we use the 0.75 efficiency factor in our calculations—it provides a necessary safety margin for temperature fluctuations.

Can I use a smaller inverter to save money?

You can, but you must check your "peak simultaneous load." If you have a microwave (1,000W) and a coffee maker (1,200W) that might both run at the same time, your inverter must be able to handle at least 2,200W of continuous load, plus the surge current required to start the motors in appliances like refrigerators.

Conclusion & Next Steps

Sizing your solar system is the most critical step in your DIY journey. By accurately auditing your energy load, understanding your local peak sun hours, and accounting for real-world inefficiencies, you can build a system that is both cost-effective and reliable.
