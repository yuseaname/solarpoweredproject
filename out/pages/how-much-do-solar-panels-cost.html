How much do solar panels cost: Complete Guide (2026)

Learn everything about how much do solar panels cost in 2026. Costs, comparisons, expert tips, and buyer's advice for US homeowners.

Updated 2026-05-26. Informational only. Consult a licensed solar installer for your specific project.

The average cost to install solar panels in the U.S. in 2026 is $2.50–$3.50 per watt before incentives, meaning a typical 6-kW residential system costs **$15,000–$21,000** out-of-pocket. After applying the **30% federal solar tax credit (ITC)**, most homeowners pay **$10,500–$14,700** — and additional state/local incentives can reduce that further.

Thinking about going solar but hesitant because of the upfront cost? You’re not alone — cost is still the #1 concern among U.S. homeowners considering solar. The good news: prices have dropped **over 70% since 2010**, and powerful incentives like the federal tax credit make solar more affordable than ever in 2026.

In this guide, you’ll get real, up-to-date pricing data for 2026 — including what’s included in the quoted price, how to estimate *your* system cost, and how to maximize savings with available incentives. We’ll also break down the hidden factors that affect value over time, like panel efficiency, warranty terms, and long-term reliability.

Whether you’re comparing quotes, researching for a future purchase, or just curious about solar economics, this 2026 cost guide gives you the facts — no fluff, no hype — so you can decide with confidence.

Cost Breakdown

2026 National Pricing Snapshot

Based on 2026 data from NREL’s latest annual report and aggregated job quotes on EnergySage, here’s what you can expect:

  • Average installed cost per watt: $2.50–$3.50/W (down from $3.10/W in 2025)
  • Typical 6-kW residential system: $15,000–$21,000 pre-incentive
  • 7-kW system with battery storage: $26,000–$36,000 (including 10 kWh battery)
  • Battery costs alone: Tesla Powerwall $11,500–$15,500; Enphase IQ Battery $4,000–$8,000 (installed)

Federal Solar Tax Credit (ITC)

The 30% federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC) is still fully in effect for systems installed between 2022 and 2032. This is not a deduction — it’s a dollar-for-dollar reduction in your federal tax bill.

For example: A $18,000 system = $5,400 credited directly to your taxes. You must have enough tax liability to claim it — but unused credits can be carried forward for up to five years.

State & Local Incentives

Many states and utilities offer extra savings:

  • California (SGIP): Up to $2/W for battery storage (income-qualified households get more)
  • New York (REsolve): Up to $5,000 for low-to-moderate income households
  • Texas (REAP grants): Local co-ops sometimes offer $200–$500/kW rebates
  • Property tax exemptions: 35+ states e xempt solar installations from property tax appraisals

Use the Database of State Incentives for Renewables & Efficiency (DSIRE) to find your local incentives.

Key Factors to Consider

System Sizing Calculator

Your electricity usage — not your roof size — should drive system size. A 6-kW system may be oversized for a small, efficient home, or undersized for a large, energy-intensive one.

Use this simple rule: Match your annual kWh usage to solar production. For example:

  1. Check your past 12 months of utility bills
  2. Divide total kWh by 1,300 (U.S. average sun hours per kW/year)
  3. Result = approximate system size in kW

Example: 12,000 kWh/year ÷ 1,300 = ~9.2 kW system

Use our Solar Sizing Calculator for a precise estimate based on your ZIP code and roof orientation.

Panel Efficiency Ratings Matter

Higher efficiency = more power per square foot — critical if your roof space is limited.

  • Premium monocrystalline panels: 22–24% efficiency (e.g., LG NeON R, Panasonic EverVolt)
  • Standard monocrystalline: 19–21% efficiency (most common mid-range option)
  • Thin-film (e.g., First Solar): 15–17% efficiency (best for large, flat commercial roofs)

Don’t just chase the highest efficiency — compare cost per watt *and* warranty. A slightly less efficient panel with a 25-year product *and* performance warranty may offer better value.

Warranty Terms: Don’t Skip This

Warranties protect your investment. Always verify:

  • Product warranty: Covers defects and workmanship. 10–15 years standard; 25+ years from top brands (e.g., SunPower, Tesla)
  • Performance warranty: Guarantees output (e.g., 92% in Year 10, 80% in Year 25)
  • Installation warranty: Most reputable installers offer 10 years on workmanship

Tip: Avoid installers whooutsource labor — if their warranty is voided by “third-party repairs,” walk away.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is solar worth it if I plan to move in 5 years?

Yes — and it may boost your home’s value. A 2025 study by Zillow found homes with solar sell for 4.1% more than comparable non-solar homes — and sell faster. Even if you don’t stay long, the ITC and lower bills provide immediate savings.

How long do solar panels last?

Modern panels are engineered for 25–30+ years — but degraded slowly. The industry standard warranty guarantees at least 80% output after 25 years. Many 1980s-era panels are still running at 60–70% capacity today.

What about maintenance?

Solar systems have virtually zero maintenance. That means:

  • No moving parts (panels don’t wear out like appliances)
  • Annual visual inspection recommended (many installers include this in warranty)
  • Cleaning rarely needed — rain usually cleans panels; only dusty/dry climates need occasional hosing

Can I add a battery later?

Absolutely. Most modern inverters (like Enphase, SolarEdge, Tesla) support battery retrofits. Adding a 10-kWh battery in 2026 costs $4,000–$8,000 extra (Enphase) or $11,500–$15,500 (Tesla Powerwall). Just ensure your inverter is hybrid-capable.

What if my roof needs replacement soon?

Replace the roof first. Solar panels last 30 years — roof shingles, 15–20. Removing and reinstalling panels to replace a roof costs $2,000–$5,000. It’s smarter (and cheaper) to roof first, then install solar.
