## Quick answer

Solar absolutely works on mobile and manufactured homes — the panels don't care what the building is. But most of the time **the roof is the wrong place to put them**: manufactured-home roofs are engineered to a lighter standard than site-built roofs, trusses are wide-spaced with little headroom to reinforce, and a penetrated roof is a permanent leak risk on a low-slope metal surface.

For most owners the right answer is a **ground-mounted array on a detached rack** — it keeps weight off a roof that wasn't designed for it, dodges the leak risk entirely, and actually harvests better because you can aim it. The honest numbers: a typical manufactured home runs **15–40 kWh per day** depending on size, HVAC, and water heater; a **3–6 kW ground-mount array** covers a meaningful share of that bill, and costs roughly **$8,000–$24,000 installed** (DIY runs about 40–60% less on equipment). The **federal 30% ITC expired December 31, 2025** — there is no federal tax credit for 2026 installs, so the payback math is just the system price against your utility bill, plus whatever your state and utility offer.

If you rent your lot, get written permission *before* spending anything, and a **portable power station plus folding panels** may be the smarter first step — zero permits, zero roof risk, and it moves with you.

## Technical considerations: Roof Integrity and Weight

This is the question that decides everything, and it's the one most mobile home owners never get a straight answer on.

The HUD code number. Manufactured homes built after June 15, 1976 follow the HUD-MCSS, and under 24 CFR 3280.305(c)(3)(i), roofs in the **North roof load zone must resist a design live load of 40 psf** on the horizontal projection (30 psf Middle, 20 psf South). That is *design* headroom, not a blank check: it's the load across the roof's design life — snow included — and manufacturers routinely build to the minimum.

**The weight, in pounds per square foot.** Do the arithmetic on a real 400W panel: the panel itself runs about **40 lb**, plus **2–5 lb per panel** of rails and clamps. A 400W panel is about 21.5 ft², so panel plus hardware lands around **2.0–2.5 psf** of local dead load — double that for wind uplift through the trusses in a storm. Not catastrophic alone, but it lands *on top of* snow, roofing, and truss weight, concentrated at attachment points, not uniform.

**Why manufactured-home roofs are special:**

- **Truss spacing and depth.** Roof trusses on manufactured homes are commonly spaced **16" on center at best, often 24"**, and the chords are sized for the HUD minimum — not for point loads bolted through the sheathing at panel corners. Solar racking wants to land on trusses; on a 24" layout that means footings only at every other truss, which increases the load per connection.
- **Low-slope metal.** Many manufactured-home roofs are near-flat or very low-slope metal. Water sheds off the panels onto a roof that has to handle more concentrated run-off, and any penetration you drill is a potential leak for the life of the home.
- **Not built for added concentrated load.** Site-built roofs under IRC have their own margins; HUD-code roofs are engineered to a lighter, more economical standard, and there is usually little attic headroom to sister trusses if the engineer says no.

**The honest engineering caveat:** *No one reading this page can tell you whether your roof passes.* The only responsible answer: **have a licensed structural engineer (or installer working with one) review the truss layout, HUD data plate, roof slope, and local wind/snow loads before any roof-mount install.** Budget a few hundred dollars for that review — the cheapest insurance in the project. If the answer is "no," that's the answer you want *before* you buy panels, not after.

For most owners, the answer "no" saves a lot of money — because the alternative is better anyway. See our <a href="/pages/ground-mount-vs-roof-mount-solar.html" class="text-link">ground mount vs roof mount comparison</a> for the full trade-off, and our <a href="/pages/ground-mount-solar-panels.html" class="text-link">ground-mount solar guide</a> for the build details.

## Why ground mount is the default recommendation

If your roof is fine and the only goal is saving money, rooftop can still work on a manufactured home whose trusses were engineered and stamped for it. But for the *typical* mobile or manufactured home, ground mounting wins on four fronts:

| Factor | Roof mount on a manufactured home | Ground mount on a detached rack |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Added roof load | Yes — 2–2.5 psf plus wind uplift at each truss | None — zero weight on the home |
| Leak risk | Penetrations through metal roof = permanent leak points | No roof contact at all |
| Orientation | Locked to how the home sits on the lot | Aim true south at latitude tilt for 5–15% more harvest |
| Repair access | Roof work, ladder, recaulking penetrations yearly | Panels at waist height, easy to clean and service |

Ground mounts cost more upfront — roughly **10–25% more than an equivalent rooftop install** — but on a manufactured home that premium buys you out of the two biggest failure modes (a sagging or leaking roof). See our <a href="/pages/ground-mount-solar-panels.html" class="text-link">ground-mount guide</a> for the footing, racking, and DIY details; a fixed-tilt galvanized rack in concrete or helical piers handles a 3–6 kW array with room to expand.

## The distance problem: detached array line loss

Move the array 50–150 feet from the home and you introduce **DC wiring loss** — the voltage drop over the run between the panels and the charge controller/inverter. Keep the math honest:

**Voltage drop (%) = (2 × one-way distance × current × wire resistance) ÷ system voltage**

Two numbers matter in practice:

- **Run length.** At 100 feet of run, a 3 kW array at 48V delivering ~62A on 10 AWG wire loses roughly **4–6%** of its harvest to heat. That's a real, permanent "tax" on everything the array produces.
- **Fix it with voltage, not just thicker wire.** Bumping the array to 48V system voltage (or using higher-Voc panels in series for grid-tied) cuts the current in half for the same power, and the loss drops with the square of the current. Run the array at the **highest practical voltage** and size the wire for **under 2–3% drop** — that is the same target our <a href="/pages/solar-wire-size.html" class="text-link">wire sizing guide</a> uses for every run.

Trenching, conduit, and wire runs roughly **$1–$4 per linear foot** installed, per our <a href="/pages/ground-mount-solar-panels.html" class="text-link">ground-mount cost breakdown</a> — a 100-foot run adds $100–$400 in materials plus a day of trenching. If the run stretches past 200 feet, step up a voltage class before buying wire; it's cheaper than the copper.

## Sizing for typical mobile-home loads

Worked example, done honestly. A typical **single-wide** uses roughly **15–20 kWh/day**; a **double-wide or park model with electric heat/HVAC runs 25–40 kWh/day** — much of it electric water heating and resistance heat in cold months. (The US household average is about 29 kWh/day; a smaller, tighter manufactured home runs below that in mild seasons, above it in winter.)

For a single-wide targeting 60% of daily use covered by solar:

- Daily target: **12 kWh/day** (20 kWh × 0.6)
- Good sun hours: **4.5** (typical US middle band)
- Array size (with 80% system efficiency): 12,000 Wh ÷ (4.5 × 0.8) ≈ **3,300W → a 3.2–3.5 kW array**
- Panels: **8–9 × 400W** panels, ~40 lb each, on a ground rack

For a double-wide targeting the same 60% off a 30 kWh/day load:

- Daily target: **18 kWh/day**
- Array: 18,000 ÷ (4.5 × 0.8) ≈ **5,000W → a 5–6 kW array**
- Panels: **12–15 × 400W** panels

## HUD code, titles, and who says yes

Two words you can't skip: **"mobile" vs "manufactured."** The difference is a legal line, not marketing:

- **Mobile home** = factory-built housing produced **before June 15, 1976**, when the HUD code took effect.
- **Manufactured home** = built **after June 15, 1976** to the HUD Manufactured Home Construction and Safety Standards (24 CFR 3280); it carries the HUD label and data plate.

That distinction matters for solar for three reasons:

1. **Data plate.** A HUD-labeled home has a plate stating the design wind zone and roof load zone — the engineer's starting point. A pre-1976 "mobile home" has no HUD data plate at all, and many have roofs nobody can certify without a full structural review. Treat pre-1976 as "roof mount almost certainly no."
2. **Who approves.** Manufactured homes are built to a *federal* standard, but **where you install** is governed by your local **AHJ — the authority having jurisdiction**: county or city planning, building, and electrical departments. Some jurisdictions treat a manufactured home's solar like any residence; others add park-community rules, setbacks, and foundation/Tie-down review. There is no one national answer — call your local building department and ask, before you pay for plans.
3. **Roof load framing.** The 20/30/40 psf roof load zone on the data plate is the number the engineer will check.

Our <a href="/pages/solar-permits-and-building-codes.html" class="text-link">solar permits and building codes guide</a> walks through the AHJ process step by step; expect a building permit (structure), an electrical permit (wiring), and — for grid-tied — utility interconnection approval. If your home sits in a community, the park's rules come *before* the AHJ's.

## Living in a rental park: permission first

If you rent the lot in a manufactured-home community, **the ground is not yours.** A permanent ground mount is an improvement to someone else's land; installing one without written permission can mean removal at your cost. Some parks also restrict roof mounts for visual reasons — and states with "solar rights" laws generally protect *homeowners*, not lot renters, so don't assume the statute covers you.

The rental-park-friendly path:

- **Get it in writing.** A letter from park management approving the array location, anchoring method, and any visual screening. No verbal "sure."
- **Use removable hardware if allowed.** A portable power station with folding panels needs no trench, no permit, no concrete — set it out on the sunny side by day, pack it inside in storms. See our <a href="/pages/portable-solar-panels.html" class="text-link">portable solar panels guide</a> for panel options and our <a href="/pages/solar-generator.html" class="text-link">solar generator explainer</a> for the battery side.
- **Size the portable system to real loads.** A 2 kWh station runs a fridge ~18 hours; a 100W panel recharges it in a day — useful for outages and recreation, not a substitute for a bill-sized array. The full fridge math is in our <a href="/pages/what-size-solar-generator-run-refrigerator.html" class="text-link">solar generator for a refrigerator guide</a>.

## Practical Strategies for Maximizing Solar Efficiency

The cheapest kilowatt-hour is the one you never use. Every watt saved shrinks the array — and on a mobile home, where the roof can't take much and the lot may be small, efficiency often decides whether the system fits at all.

### 1. Upgrade to LED lighting

Old incandescent and CFL bulbs are straight waste. Swapping to LEDs cuts lighting draw by **up to 80%** — a $50–150 project and the fastest shrink you'll get.

### 2. Seal the leaks

Mobile homes are notoriously leaky around windows, doors, skirting, and utility penetrations. Weatherstripping, caulk, and skirting repair tighten the envelope so the HVAC doesn't run overtime — and HVAC is the biggest load in the budget. Sealing before sizing can drop daily kWh by 10–25% in extreme months, which is exactly what decides between a 5 kW array and a 3 kW one.

### 3. Fix the two killers: water heater and heat

Electric resistance water heating and electric heat are the loads that blow up mobile-home solar budgets. A heat-pump water heater can cut water-heating energy roughly in half, and a heat pump (instead of resistance strips) does the same for heating — the largest single upgrade available to a manufactured home before or after solar.

### 4. Watch your usage

A $30–50 energy monitor (or your utility's usage portal) shows which appliances draw at night, when the array is dead. Shift laundry, dishwashing, and water heating to solar hours where possible — behavior is free capacity.

## FAQ

{{< faq "Is solar power legal on a mobile or manufactured home?" >}}
Legally, yes — manufactured homes are dwellings, and most jurisdictions permit solar with the same building, electrical, and interconnection approvals used for site-built homes. The two real gates are structural (can the roof take it) and the AHJ's rules in your county or city; if you rent your lot, park permission comes first.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can I put solar panels on a mobile home roof without an engineer?" >}}
Not responsibly. Manufactured-home roofs are engineered to HUD minimums (20/30/40 psf of design live load by zone), and no one can tell you if your trusses have spare capacity without reviewing the data plate, truss layout, and local wind/snow loads. A structural review costs a few hundred dollars; a roof failure costs far more. Ground mounting dodges the question entirely.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How much solar do I need for a mobile home?" >}}
Measure, don't guess: your utility bill shows kWh/day (divide monthly by 30). A single-wide at 20 kWh/day targeting 60% offset needs roughly a 3.2–3.5 kW array at 4.5 good sun hours; a double-wide at 30 kWh/day needs about 5–6 kW. Run our worked examples, then confirm with a load calculation.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What's the difference between a mobile home and a manufactured home?" >}}
The legal line is June 15, 1976, when the HUD code took effect. Homes built before that date are "mobile homes" with no HUD data plate; homes built after are "manufactured homes" built to 24 CFR 3280, with a HUD label stating wind and roof-load zones. For solar, the difference matters because the data plate is the starting point for any roof structural review — and pre-1976 roofs rarely pass one.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can I use a portable power station instead of a full solar install?" >}}
Yes, and in a rental park it's often the better first step — no permits, no roof penetrations, no trenching, and it covers outages, recreation, and fridge backup. A 2 kWh station runs a fridge about 18 hours, but a 100W panel recharges it in about a day; for bill-scale offset you still need a fixed array.
{{< /faq >}}
