## Quick answer

This hub maps the site's solar scenarios — home backup, off-grid cabins, RVs and vans, sheds and outbuildings, yard lighting, phone-level charging, water heating, and mobile homes — to the specific guides that solve each one. Every section gives the honest scale, a budget band pulled from our cost breakdowns, and the mistake that most often wrecks that scenario. Pick your scenario and follow its links.

## Key takeaways

- **Every scenario shares one first step:** count daily watt-hours (Wh/day) before buying anything — a fridge backup, a cabin, and an RV differ in scale, not method.
- **Budget bands are wide because loads are personal:** roughly $500–$3,000 for a shed, $1,500–$4,000 for a realistic RV build, $1,000–$9,000 for a cabin depending on tier.
- **The battery is almost always the biggest line item** — often 30–40% of a small-system budget — so load counting saves more than panel shopping.
- **The 30% federal tax credit expired December 31, 2025** (P.L. 119-21). 2026 installs get $0 federal credit, so payback math uses the full system price — roughly 10–13 years in high-cost states.
- **DIY saves real money on small systems** — commonly 30–50% on RV-scale builds — but grid-tied work usually needs permits and a licensed electrician.

## Home backup: critical loads, not the whole house

A realistic home backup keeps a fridge, lights, phones, a CPAP, and maybe a well pump alive — roughly 1,500–4,000 Wh/day. A 2,000Wh-class portable station runs about $1,500–$2,200; a wired home battery runs roughly $1,000–$1,400 per kWh installed before panels. The biggest mistake is sizing for the whole house; pick critical loads first and the budget shrinks an order of magnitude. With no federal credit for 2026 installs, backup buys resilience, not payback — the [federal tax credit explainer](/pages/solar-panel-tax-credit.html) covers what remains.

{{< faq "How big a battery do I need to run just my fridge overnight through an outage?" >}}
A modern fridge at 150W and 40% duty cycle uses 150 × 0.40 × 24 = 1,440 Wh/day. At 85% usable capacity that is 1,440 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 1,700 Wh — a 2,000Wh-class station covers about one day; double it for a realistic multi-day outage.
{{< /faq >}}

## Cabins and off-grid homes: winter is the design case

Cabin systems live or die on the worst week, not the average day — a mid-range build with a fridge runs $3,000–$9,000; a lights-and-charging starter lands at $1,000–$3,000. The classic mistake is sizing panels for July sun and watching the bank starve in December, when sun hours drop by half or more. Design to winter Wh/day; treat summer surplus as a bonus. The [off-grid system setup guide](/pages/off-grid-solar-system-setup-guide.html) walks the full build order.

{{< faq "Can a small cabin system run a full-size refrigerator?" >}}
Yes, but it sets your floor. The fridge alone needs ~1,440 Wh/day; add lights, a water pump, and a laptop (80 + 240 + 240 Wh) and you are near 2,000 Wh/day: 2,000 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 2,350 Wh of battery and 2,000 ÷ (4 × 0.8) = 625W of panels in a 4-sun-hour climate — a $3,000–$9,000 mid-range build, not a $1,000 starter kit.
{{< /faq >}}

## RVs and vans: battery-forward, surge-aware

RV systems are compact, 12V-centric, and battery-dominated — typically 30–40% of total cost — with realistic daily-use builds at $1,500–$4,000 and weekend kits from about $700. The biggest mistake is expecting 400–800W of roof panels to run a 13,500BTU air conditioner; that load alone can exceed the entire array's output. Solar covers the fridge, fans, lights, and electronics; cooling comes from shore power, a generator, or discipline. The [RV solar sizing guide](/pages/rv-solar-sizing.html) shows the load math; the [12V vs 24V vs 48V guide](/pages/12v-vs-24v-vs-48v-solar.html) explains why RVs stay at 12V.

{{< faq "Will 400W of panels run my RV air conditioner?" >}}
No. A 15,000BTU RV AC draws roughly 1,300–1,800W running, so a 400W array covers only a fraction even at peak sun, and the start surge exceeds most RV inverters. Panels keep the house battery and 12V fridge happy; cooling needs another source.

## Sheds and outbuildings: the best first project

A shed is the cheapest place to learn solar because the loads are small and forgiving — a security-shed setup (LED light, router, camera) runs about 530 Wh/day, a light workshop about 560 Wh/day, and complete systems typically $500–$3,000. The biggest mistake is underestimating surge and wire size: motor starts and long wire runs punish undersized components. Most sheds are electrically isolated, so an off-grid DC-coupled setup avoids trenching and permits entirely.

{{< faq "What size solar setup does a basic shed need?" >}}
For the 530 Wh/day security shed: panels = 530 ÷ (4 × 0.8) ≈ 166W, so a 200W array covers it with margin; battery = 530 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 625 Wh — one 12V 100Ah AGM (1,200Wh) at 50% depth of discharge, or one 100Ah LiFePO4 with room to grow.
{{< /faq >}}

## Yards and gardens: cheap wins, with a quality floor

Yard solar is the lowest-stakes scenario here — quality path and accent lights run $25–$60 per unit with zero operating cost, so a six-light yard lands around $150–$360, with payback versus wired lighting within one to two growing seasons. The mistake is buying $5 units: low-grade batteries and thin housings that fade in a single season, which is why our guide draws a quality floor near $40 with LiFePO4 cells and IP65 sealing. Shading matters most here — yards are where trees and fences live.

{{< faq "Are cheap solar garden lights worth it?" >}}
For decoration, sometimes; for path or security lighting, no. A $5 light's NiMH batteries and thin plastic degrade in one season, while a $40–$60 unit with LiFePO4 cells and IP65 sealing lasts several years — the cheap option usually costs more per year of service.
{{< /faq >}}

## Phones and small devices: the entry point, with honest limits

Personal solar is a $20–$120 decision: 5W integrated chargers ($20–$50) suit an emergency glovebox; 10–28W foldables ($40–$120) are the real camping tool. The most common failure is arithmetic, not hardware — if your phone draws 5W running GPS and the panel delivers 4W in shade, the battery never charges. Buy wattage above your worst-case draw; cloud cuts output 50–80%, so higher-wattage panels keep working in bad weather.

<a href="/pages/solar-phone-charger.html" class="text-link">Solar phone charger guide</a> — panel classes, the $15–$200 tier ladder, and why phones refuse to charge in shade.

<a href="/pages/portable-solar-panels.html" class="text-link">Portable solar panels</a> — foldables that pair with power banks and small stations.

{{< faq "Why won't my phone charge from a solar panel on a cloudy day?" >}}
Thin cloud cuts output 50–80%, and a phone drawing 5W needs the panel to exceed that just to start charging. A 5W panel in shade delivers 1–2W — below the draw — while a 21W panel keeps enough headroom to charge through it.
{{< /faq >}}

## Water heating: solar thermal, a different technology

Solar water heating is not PV — collectors heat fluid directly at 60–70% efficiency versus 15–22% for panels, and installed systems run $3,000–$7,000 with typical payback in 5–10 years by displacing 50–80% of water-heating energy. The biggest mistake is pricing a PV array when you wanted thermal. Flat-plate collectors suit moderate climates at $2,000–$4,000; evacuated tubes cost $3,500–$7,000 and hold performance in cold, cloudy regions.

<a href="/pages/solar-water-heater.html" class="text-link">Solar water heater guide</a> — collector types, components, and the full cost-and-payback breakdown.

<a href="/pages/solar-payback-calculator.html" class="text-link">Solar payback calculator</a> — run thermal-versus-PV payback with your actual utility rate.

{{< faq "Is a solar water heater better than solar panels for hot water?" >}}
Per square foot, yes — thermal captures 60–70% of incident solar energy versus 15–22% for PV, so less roof area produces the same heat. But thermal does one job, needs freeze protection in cold climates, and runs $3,000–$7,000 installed, while PV offsets every load in the house. Water heating only: thermal wins. Whole-house offset: PV is the flexible choice.
{{< /faq >}}

## Mobile homes: feasible, but check the roof first

Solar works on manufactured homes, but the roof is the gate: many were built with lighter framing never rated for panel dead load plus wind load, so a structural inspection comes before any quote. Most park-set homes go grid-tied like site-built houses — with no federal credit for 2026 installs, payback in high-rate states runs roughly 10–13 years on the full system price — while truly mobile setups use ground mounts. The biggest mistake is penetrating a thin metal roof without engineered waterproofing details.

{{< faq "Can you put solar panels on a mobile home roof?" >}}
Often, but not always safely. Panels and racking add dead plus wind load that older or thin-roofed units may not carry, so the sequence is inspection first, reinforcement if needed, then mounting — and ground mounting sidesteps roof limits entirely.

## FAQ

{{< faq "What is the one sizing step every use case shares?" >}}
Count daily watt-hours: watts × hours of use per device, summed. Every panel, battery, and inverter size above derives from that number. It takes 20 minutes with a plug-in meter or nameplate estimate.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Is there still a federal solar tax credit for 2026 installations?" >}}
No. The 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit expired for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, under P.L. 119-21. 2026 installs get $0 federal credit, so payback math uses the full system price — roughly 10–13 years in high-cost states — while state credits, tax exemptions, and utility rebates still reduce real cost.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Which use case is the cheapest way to start with solar?" >}}
Yard lighting and phone charging, in that order — both under a few hundred dollars with no wiring or permits. A shed system is the cheapest *project* that teaches real sizing skills — same architecture as a cabin or RV at a fraction of the cost.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can one system serve several of these use cases?" >}}
Yes, if you size for the largest scenario. A cabin-scale battery and 600W array covers shed- and device-scale loads with headroom; the reverse is not true. Start from your biggest Wh/day scenario and let smaller ones ride along.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How much can I save by building it myself?" >}}
On RV- and shed-scale systems, DIY commonly saves 30–50% versus professional installation. Grid-tied work is different: permits, inspections, and structural sign-off usually make a licensed installer the practical path.
{{< /faq >}}
